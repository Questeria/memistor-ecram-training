In 1960 Widrow and Hoff stored the weights of their adaptive neuron in memistors, three-terminal electrochemical cells whose conductance is set by the charge through a third terminal; their modern descendants are lithium-ion, metal-oxide and protonic ECRAM. We ask, in simulation, whether such devices can train neural networks in memory to near-digital accuracy, what specifications they need, and what energy advantage survives once the periphery is counted. Our device model (levels, asymmetry, saturation, write noise, spreads, drift, reference error) reproduces a published analog-training result: 1.78%, 14.47% and 2.26% MNIST test error for the exact update, SGD and Tiki-Taka on the RPU-baseline device, against 2.0%, about 15% and about 2%. In a pre-registered comparison (five seeds) of four published device specifications and Widrow's memistor on MNIST, a CIFAR-10 subset and a character language model on human-written text, plain SGD fails on every published device, while Tiki-Taka-family algorithms bring the Li-ECRAM and protonic specifications to the baseline within our gap lines or within five-seed resolution on all three tasks. With per-arm learning rates the gap does not grow with width. Sensitivity sweeps show that an accurate symmetry-point reference and a saturating response are the binding specifications, while a few hundred levels suffice. Counting the periphery at a measured analog-AI chip's efficiency, training energy per weight is about 32x below an H100 at its measured utilisation (15-234x over our sensitivity range); the read periphery, not the device, dominates. We release the simulator, all records, a device-experiment roadmap with pass criteria and a bench replication of the 1960 memistor.
